You know that little map that pops up when you search for something like “plumber near me” or “coffee shop Red Bank”? Those top three listings, with the star ratings and the phone number right there—that’s the local pack. And I’ll be honest: if your business isn’t in it, you’re leaving a whole lot of phone calls and foot traffic on the table.

For a small business in Monmouth County, your Google Business Profile—what used to be called Google My Business—isn’t some optional extra. It’s arguably as important as your actual website. Sometimes more. Because someone standing in a parking lot in Freehold, phone in one hand, searching for a roofer, isn’t going to scroll through pages of results. They’re going to tap the first name they see that looks trustworthy and close. If that name isn’t yours, you never even got a chance.

I learned that lesson a few years back through a friend of mine—let’s call him Paul. Paul runs a small landscaping company out of Howell. He’s good at what he does. His yards look immaculate, his pricing is fair, and his clients love him. But for the longest time, his phone wasn’t ringing the way it should. He was spending money on a decent website and even a little on ads, but his GBP listing was a ghost town. No photos besides the blurry shot Google pulled from Street View. No posts. A handful of scattered reviews, some of them unanswered. He had the wrong category selected—something generic like “Landscaper” instead of “Landscape designer” or “Lawn care service.” He was practically invisible for searches like “landscaping Howell NJ” that his competitors were cleaning up on.

We sat down one afternoon and systematically worked through his profile. I’m going to walk you through exactly what we did, because it’s the same stuff that can turn a silent phone into a steady stream of local leads. Paul’s story isn’t unique. It’s the quiet reality for a lot of Monmouth County business owners who just haven’t given their GBP the attention it deserves.

What exactly does “optimizing your Google Business Profile” mean?

Optimizing your GBP isn’t about tricking the system. It’s about making your profile as complete, accurate, and active as possible so that Google feels confident showing it to people nearby. Unlike the regular blue-link search results, Google Maps and the local pack lean heavily on three things: proximity (how close you are to the searcher), relevance (does your profile clearly say what you do and where you do it), and what I’ll call trust signals (reviews, photos, regular activity, consistent information across the web).

The beautiful part is that a small shop in Red Bank can outrank a much larger competitor from out of town simply by having a more detailed, more active profile. The playing field isn’t exactly level, but it’s a lot flatter than you might think. All it takes is a little deliberate effort and a willingness to stick with it.

Why this matters so much for Monmouth County businesses

Think about the last time you needed something quickly—a plumber for a leaky pipe, a dentist for a sudden toothache, a takeout spot for dinner. Chances are you pulled out your phone, typed a few words, and scanned the map results. Most people do that. And they almost never scroll past the first three or four listings. Those top spots are where the calls go.

In a county with towns as tightly packed as Freehold, Middletown, Long Branch, and Asbury Park, local search is intensely competitive. Someone in Eatontown might see a completely different set of results than someone in Hazlet, even for the same search. The businesses that win are the ones Google trusts the most. And Google trusts profiles that look alive and well cared for.

If your website isn’t pulling its weight in search, that’s a whole other conversation—I wrote about why your site might not be ranking in NJ in another post—but the GBP is often the quicker, more immediate lever you can pull. It’s the front door to your business online. You want it looking sharp.

1. Choose the right primary category (it’s not a guessing game)

Your primary category tells Google what your business fundamentally is. It’s one of the strongest signals you can send. And a surprising number of businesses pick something vague or just wrong. Paul, my landscaper friend, had “Landscaper” selected. That’s fine, but it’s broad. When we switched his primary category to “Landscape designer”—which more closely matched the high-end work he actually did—his visibility for those specific searches jumped noticeably within a few weeks.

Pick the category that most accurately describes your core offering. If you’re a roofer, choose “Roofing contractor,” not just “Contractor.” If you’re a web designer, pick “Web designer,” not “Marketing agency.” You can add secondary categories for the other things you do, but your primary category should be razor sharp. Don’t try to be everything at once. Be the thing you do best.

2. Fill out every single field (most people don’t)

I can’t tell you how many profiles I’ve looked at where the business hours are missing, the description is blank, and the service area is undefined. Those profiles rarely rank well because they look incomplete—and Google takes that as a signal that you’re not really trying.

Go through your profile and fill in your business name exactly as it appears in the real world. No keyword stuffing at the end like “Bob’s Plumbing Freehold NJ 24/7.” That can get your listing suspended. Instead, just use your real name. Add your physical address if you serve customers there, or define your service area if you travel to clients. Make sure your phone number is correct and consistent with the one on your website. Set your hours—and update them for holidays. Write a description that naturally mentions what you do, who you serve, and the towns you cover. Something like:

“We’re a family-owned roofing company based in Freehold, serving all of Monmouth County including Marlboro, Manalapan, and Howell. From small repairs to full replacements, we’ve got you covered.”

That kind of description reads like a human wrote it. And it contains location and service keywords without sounding like a robot.

3. Add services—but don’t just list them; describe them

The services section is often overlooked. Business owners might add a few one-word entries like “Roof Repair” and call it a day. But every service entry is a tiny piece of real estate you can use to signal relevance.

Paul and I rewrote his services. Instead of “Lawn Mowing,” we put “Weekly Lawn Mowing in Howell and Freehold.” Instead of “Mulching,” we wrote “Seasonal Mulching and Garden Bed Cleanup for Monmouth County Homes.” The difference in tone and specificity is night and day. Just don’t spam. Keep it natural, but be intentional. Mention the towns you actually work in. That helps Google connect your profile to local searches.

4. Get more reviews—and talk back to the ones you get

Reviews are a monster ranking factor. It’s not just the number of stars; Google also looks at how frequently you get reviews, whether they contain relevant keywords, and whether you, the business owner, take the time to respond.

Every time you finish a job or serve a happy customer, ask them—politely, directly—to leave you a Google review. Send them a link. Make it easy. And when they do leave a review, respond. Not just a canned “Thank you,” but a genuine reply that mentions their name, the service, and the location.

For example: “Thanks, Sarah, for trusting us with your kitchen renovation in Red Bank! It was a pleasure working with you.” That one sentence tells Google you’re active, you’re legitimate, and you’re definitely in Red Bank. It’s a small thing with an outsized impact.

Paul started asking every client for a review after a project wrapped. Within a couple of months, he went from 7 reviews to over 30, and his response rate was 100%. His ranking for “landscaping Howell NJ” climbed from page three to the top of the local pack. That’s not coincidence.

5. Add photos regularly (and make them real)

Google loves fresh photos. Profiles with frequent image uploads get a ranking boost because they signal an active, legitimate business. But don’t just throw up a few stock photos of smiling models holding wrenches. Post real pictures of your work, your team, your truck, your shop. Before-and-after shots are gold—they tell a story and give people a reason to click.

Paul started taking quick photos of every finished project with his phone. Nothing fancy, just honest shots of transformed yards. He’d upload them once a week. It took five minutes. That steady stream of activity kept his profile feeling alive, and the photos often showed up directly in the search results, giving him an edge over competitors whose profiles looked frozen in 2019.

6. Post updates like you would on social media

The Google Business Profile has a “Posts” feature that’s criminally underused. You can share updates, offers, events, or even just a link to a recent blog post. These posts don’t stick around forever—they expire after a while—but while they’re live, they show that your business is active and engaged. They also give you another place to mention your town and services.

You can repurpose content you’re already creating. Wrote a blog post about roof maintenance? Turn it into a short GBP post with a link. Just finished a big project in Asbury Park? Share a photo and a one-sentence caption. It’s low-effort and it feeds Google’s appetite for fresh signals.

7. Build local citations (consistency is everything)

Your business’s name, address, and phone number—often called NAP—need to be identical everywhere they appear online. On your website, on Facebook, on Yelp, on local directories. If your address shows up as “123 Main St.” in one place and “123 Main Street” in another, Google gets a tiny bit less confident that your business is the same one. Over time, those inconsistencies chip away at your trustworthiness.

Sit down and make sure your NAP is clean. There are tools that can help with this, but even a manual check of the top directories and social platforms goes a long way. For Paul, we discovered his old phone number was still listed on a couple of old directories from years ago. Cleaning those up took an afternoon, but it tightened up his whole online presence.

If your website is part of the problem—outdated info, no location pages—it’s worth addressing that too. I outlined the bigger picture in our local SEO guide for Ocean and Monmouth County businesses, which covers how your site and your GBP can work together.

8. Connect your website and your GBP so they talk to each other

Your website and your Google Business Profile should feel like two parts of the same story. When someone lands on your site, they should immediately see the same business name, the same phone number, the same sense of place. If your contact page says one thing and your GBP says another, it creates friction.

Make sure your site has a dedicated page for each major service area or town you cover. If you serve Freehold, Howell, and Colts Neck, having pages on your site that talk about the work you’ve done in those towns reinforces everything your GBP is signaling. Link those pages in your posts occasionally. It’s not about gaming the system; it’s about building a coherent web of signals that all point to the same truth: this is a real business, doing real work, right here in Monmouth County.

9. Optimize for multiple towns (without doing anything shady)

If your business serves several towns in the county, you might be tempted to create multiple GBP listings—one for each town. Don’t do it. That’s against Google’s guidelines and can get your profile suspended. Instead, build that multi-town presence organically.

Use your GBP posts to mention specific towns. Upload photos tagged with locations when possible. Encourage reviews from customers in different towns, and respond to them by name-dropping the location. Over time, Google picks up on those patterns.

Paul, for instance, started asking his clients in Freehold to mention the town in their reviews, and he’d do the same in his replies. Within a few months, he was showing up for “landscaping Freehold NJ” almost as strongly as he was for Howell. No tricks—just consistency.

What actually moves the needle (a short list, because I know you’re busy)

If you’re feeling overwhelmed, here’s where to put your energy first. These five things account for the vast majority of ranking improvement I’ve seen:

  1. Get your primary category right.
  2. Collect reviews regularly, and respond to every one.
  3. Keep your profile complete—nothing left blank.
  4. Stay active with photos and posts, even just a little bit each week.
  5. Make sure your website mirrors your profile information and supports your locations.

That alone will put you ahead of most Monmouth County competitors who set their profile up once in 2018 and forgot the password.

The bottom line

Ranking in the Google Maps pack isn’t some mysterious black box. It’s the natural byproduct of relevance, trust, and consistency. The businesses that win aren’t necessarily the biggest or the oldest. They’re the ones that look like they care—about their online presence, about their customers, about showing up with honest, helpful information.

Paul’s landscaping business did a complete turnaround. His phone started ringing with calls from people who found him on the map. That steady stream of local leads gave him the confidence to hire another crew member. All from a few hours of attention to a free Google profile. The same can happen for you.

If your profile is gathering digital dust and your phone isn’t ringing the way it used to, it’s almost certainly fixable. You don’t need a massive budget—just some guidance and a little follow-through.

Frequently asked questions

How long does it take to see an improvement in Google Business Profile rankings?

You might see small shifts within a couple of weeks, especially if you were missing obvious things like a category or a description. But consistent, durable results typically take between one and three months, depending on how competitive your area is and how active your competitors are.

Do reviews really make that much of a difference?

Yes. They’re one of the heaviest local ranking factors. It’s not just the star rating; the frequency of new reviews and the presence of keywords in the review text and your responses all matter. A business with 25 recent reviews and active replies will almost always outrank one with 100 old, ignored reviews.

Can I rank in multiple towns without multiple locations?

Absolutely. The key is building relevance signals for each town through your website content, GBP posts, and the reviews you get from customers in those places. It’s a slower approach, but it’s the one that won’t get you penalized.

Is a Google Business Profile enough, even without a website?

You can get some visibility, but long-term, you’re capping your potential. A website that reinforces your GBP with deeper content—service pages, local landing pages, blog posts—creates a virtuous circle. It’s the difference between a listing and a genuine local brand. I’d recommend using them together.

What’s the single biggest mistake business owners make with their GBP?

Inactivity. They set it up once, maybe add a logo, and then never touch it again. An inactive profile tells Google you’re not engaged, and it’s incredibly easy for an active competitor to leapfrog you. Just a few minutes a week can make a world of difference.
